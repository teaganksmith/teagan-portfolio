Quantifying the Frequency and Distribution of Beluga Whale Scars Through Photo Identification in the Churchill River

Arctic Field Biology Course, University of Toronto Mississauga & University of Manitoba · Churchill, MB · July – August 2023 · Supervisors: Peter Kotanen, Stephen Peterson

What is the frequency and body distribution of anthropogenic scars in the Churchill River beluga subpopulation, and can scar patterns indicate source of injury?

My Role

I conducted boat surveys along the Churchill River, photographing surfacing beluga pods using DSLR cameras. I built and applied a systematic photo identification classification system to assess scar placement across seven body segments, analysing 5,642 photos from a catalogue of 10,239. I also produced the beluga body segment illustration used in the study.

Collaborators & Institutional Partners

Beluga Bits citizen science initiative · Assiniboine Park Zoo · Polar Bear International · University of Toronto Mississauga · University of Manitoba

Methods

Ten boat surveys over approximately four hours each covered a 9.76 km by 6.66 km range at the mouth of the Churchill River. A seven-segment body classification system was developed from existing literature to assess scar distribution across the dorso-lateral surface of surfacing individuals. Analysis was restricted to adult belugas with presumed anthropogenic scarring, excluding neonates, subadults, dermatological conditions, and confirmed naturally occurring scars. Statistical analysis used two-way ANOVA and post-hoc Tukey-Kramer tests in R.

Key Findings

Of scarred adults, 63.63% carried presumed anthropogenic scars. Entanglement was the most frequently identified source (26.86%), followed by harvest-related injury (12.5%) and vessel strikes (10.23%). Scar distribution varied significantly across body segments (p = 0.005), consistent with entanglement scars concentrating in the dorsal and anterior peduncle regions. No significant difference was found between left and right lateral sides. Survivor bias is a confirmed limitation: analysis was restricted to live animals, likely underrepresenting the true frequency and severity of anthropogenic injury.
