I'm a science writer, researcher, and artist whose work keeps returning to the same question: what is it like to be an animal?

My writing is drawn to the places where scientific evidence collides with philosophical uncertainty — where we have data on animal behaviour but still argue about what it means, where the technology to decode non-human communication exists but the interpretive frameworks don't yet. I cover animal cognition, interspecies communication, bioacoustics, and the legal and ethical implications of what we're learning about non-human minds. The beat is specific, but the questions underneath it are ancient: Who gets to count as someone? What does it cost us not to know?

My research background is in ecology and evolutionary biology at the University of Toronto, where I completed an Honours BSc with a 3.9 GPA. I was funded by the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada — nationally competitive federal research funding from Canada's primary science funding agency — to conduct evolutionary genomics research in the Sage Lab. My field research took me to Churchill, Manitoba, where I designed and ran an independent study on anthropogenic disturbance to beluga whale pods during their Hudson Bay migration, integrating acoustic monitoring, boat-based transect surveys, and remote camera analysis. I also conducted spatial ecology work with the Darling Lab, analysing global coral reef health data and the geographic bias of marine conservation science. In September 2026, I begin the Erasmus Mundus MSc GLOBE programme — a competitive pan-European research degree in Global Environmental Sciences.

I'm building toward placement at recognised science editorial venues. My Substack, [newsletter name], covers animal cognition and interspecies communication for a scientifically literate general audience — five pieces published so far, more in draft. The kinds of stories I want to tell sit at the intersection of primary research and public stakes: the AI-decoded sperm whale coda that edges us toward something like language; the Hawaiian crow that lost vocal repertoire elements in captivity and what that means for conservation; the legal question of whether a whale can have standing.

My art practice grows from the same fieldwork and observation that informs the writing. Scientific data — spectrograms, field notes, specimen study — feed directly into the studio work. The art asks what is left over after measurement: the quality of attention, the texture of a sound, what a subject looks like when it looks back. I'm based in Toronto, which means I am frequently found at the ROM's ornithology collection and always planning the next field season.
